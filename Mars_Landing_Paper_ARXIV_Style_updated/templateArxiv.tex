\begin{abstract}
Onboard hazard detection for planetary landing must run on radiation-hardened processors with tight compute and memory budgets. Single-frame 2D optical sensing is the simplest and cheapest sensing option available, and passive optical hazard avoidance has flown on recent lunar landers. We characterize the operational bounds of a deterministic, single-frame hazard detection pipeline built on image intensity gradients and morphological shadow augmentation, evaluated against slope-derived proxy labels (unsafe: slope $>15^\circ$) on geographically disjoint Martian test sites. Raw intensity gradients produce an inverted hazard response in shadowed crater bowls (AUROC 0.350), a failure mode we trace to vanishing gradients under single-direction illumination. Shadow augmentation corrects this inversion (AUROC 0.796, PR-AUC 0.559, nearly six times the 0.095 chance level), and the front-end runs in 74.0~ms at native resolution with under 16~MB of peak memory. We then treat the lander footprint requirement as an explicit operating parameter rather than a fixed constant. For a $25\text{ m} \times 25\text{ m}$ requirement, the selected region meets the area requirement on 68.8\% of held-out tiles, but because the regions are elongated, only 7.5\% of tiles yield a region that contains a $25\text{ m} \times 25\text{ m}$ square with at most 5\% unsafe terrain. This safe landing rate reaches 50\% only for footprints up to 9~m, never exceeds 60\% at any footprint because every rocky-terrain region and 12 of 20 crater regions exceed the 5\% tolerance, and is zero above 31~m; no tile supports a $50\text{ m} \times 50\text{ m}$ requirement. Single-frame 2D sensing therefore has a narrow operating envelope, limited to footprints of order 10~m, and beyond it is better suited as a low-cost cueing stage for 3D sensors; we quantify where that envelope ends.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous planetary landers operate under extreme communication latency, necessitating onboard hazard detection and avoidance systems that execute on radiation-hardened flight processors with severe computational constraints. While deep learning methods excel in terrestrial computer vision, classical lightweight vision pipelines remain strong candidates for spaceflight deployment because they are deterministic, interpretable, and cheap to verify. Landing-site selection for missions such as the Mars Science Laboratory relied on extensive orbital reconnaissance and terrain analysis prior to entry \cite{golombek2012selection}, while real-time onboard hazard detection has been pursued through lidar-based sensing \cite{amzajerdian2013lidar}, learning-based detection \cite{moghe2020deep}, and vision-based navigation systems such as the Mars 2020 Lander Vision System \cite{johnson2020lvs} and NASA's next-generation hazard detection system \cite{restrepo2020hds}. Radiation-hardened flight processors, such as the BAE RAD750 \cite{berger2001rad750}, provide limited compute throughput and memory bandwidth.

3D sensors such as lidar measure slope directly but add mass, power, and integration cost. A single camera, often already carried for terrain-relative navigation, is the simplest alternative. The practical question is therefore not whether 2D optical sensing is as good as 3D sensing, but \emph{under which operating conditions} it is good enough. Two conditions matter most: the terrain and illumination encountered, and the size of the hazard-free footprint the vehicle needs. A small lander or rotorcraft that needs a few tens of metres of clear ground places very different demands on a hazard map than a large lander that needs $50$~m or more.

Hazard detection algorithms are frequently evaluated against proxy ground truth derived from orbital Digital Terrain Models (DTMs). Such evaluations require careful class definition: treating the dominant safe terrain as the positive class allows high accuracy by trivially predicting the majority class and masks dangerous false negatives. We therefore evaluate unsafe terrain as the primary class throughout.

This paper characterizes the operating envelope of single-frame, intensity-based hazard scoring. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A leak-free evaluation protocol with site-level geographic separation between development and test terrain and unsafe terrain as the primary evaluation class.
    \item Identification of a physical failure mode, an \emph{inverted} hazard response of raw intensity gradients in shadowed craters, and a lightweight shadow augmentation step that corrects it (AUROC $0.350 \rightarrow 0.796$).
    \item A footprint-requirement sweep that maps landing-region availability as a function of the required lander footprint, identifying the footprint sizes at which single-frame 2D sensing is and is not sufficient.
    \item Per-stage latency and memory profiles showing that the front-end fits comfortably within flight-class compute budgets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Passive optical hazard detection.} The closest prior work is the multi-cue hazard detection system of Huertas, Cheng and Madison \cite{huertas2006passive}, which combines shadow, crater, slope and texture cues extracted from passive imagery to detect landing hazards for Mars and other landers. Our pipeline is a deliberately minimal member of this family: a single gradient cue and a single shadow cue, chosen so that its cost and its failure modes can be measured in isolation. Shadow analysis is also an established orbital tool: automated detection of rock shadows in HiRISE images was used to estimate rock abundance and select the Phoenix landing site \cite{golombek2008rocks}, a setting where illumination geometry is known and processing time is not constrained.

\textbf{Flown optical hazard avoidance.} Single-camera hazard detection has already flown. Chang'e-3 used an optical coarse hazard avoidance stage during powered descent, followed by fine avoidance with a 3D laser imaging sensor while hovering \cite{li2016change3}; single-image methods for selecting landing areas in this optical coarse stage are described in \cite{wei2018landing}. Chandrayaan-3 carried a Lander Hazard Detection and Avoidance Camera with an onboard processing algorithm among its lander sensors \cite{isro2023ch3}. These missions establish that passive optical sensing is operationally useful for coarse avoidance; our study does not argue otherwise, but quantifies, for one simple classical pipeline, how that usefulness depends on the required landing footprint and what it costs computationally.

\textbf{3D sensing.} Lidar-based hazard detection, developed in NASA's ALHAT project \cite{epp2008alhat,amzajerdian2013lidar} and its successors \cite{restrepo2020hds}, measures slope and roughness directly and remains the reference approach for precise avoidance. For planetary rotorcraft, Schoppmann et al. \cite{schoppmann2021multi} build multi-resolution elevation maps from monocular structure-from-motion and select landing sites by slope and roughness; this recovers 3D structure from multiple frames, whereas our pipeline uses a single frame and no motion.

\textbf{Learning-based hazard detection.} Convolutional networks have been trained to segment hazards for lunar landing \cite{moghe2020deep}, and Bayesian segmentation networks add per-pixel uncertainty that can be used to filter candidate sites, evaluated on terrain simulated from HiRISE DTMs \cite{tomita2022bayesian}. These methods are typically more accurate than hand-designed cues but require training data and substantially more onboard computation. Our focus is the opposite end of this trade-off: a deterministic pipeline with no learned parameters, whose accuracy we report alongside its runtime and memory footprint.

\section{Dataset and Terrain Independence}
We evaluate our method on a synthetic Martian dataset comprising 160 rendered tiles ($768 \times 768$ pixels at 1.0~m Ground Sampling Distance) across four distinct geological site types: craters, rocky terrain, smooth plains, and dunes. The imagery is rendered under a standard Lambertian illumination model with solar azimuth $45^\circ$ and solar elevation $30^\circ$, while the reference topography represents high-resolution orbital DTM geometry exemplified by HiRISE \cite{mcewen2007hirise}. Proxy hazard labels are defined from the local gradient of the underlying DTM: any pixel with a maximum local slope exceeding $15^\circ$ over a 5~m baseline is labeled proxy-unsafe. Across the dataset, the average proxy-safe fraction is 90.2\%, meaning the hazardous unsafe class has a baseline prevalence of 9.8\% (and 9.5\% on the held-out test split).

\textbf{Terrain Independence:} To ensure leak-free evaluation, train/validation and test independence is guaranteed via geographic site-level partitioning rather than random patch shuffling. The development split (DEV, 80 tiles) is drawn exclusively from four distinct geographic regions (\texttt{crater\_1}, \texttt{rocky\_1}, \texttt{smooth\_1}, and \texttt{dunes\_1}). The held-out test split (TEST, 80 tiles) comprises four entirely separate geographic regions (\texttt{crater\_2}, \texttt{rocky\_2}, \texttt{smooth\_2}, and \texttt{dunes\_2}), generated using independent pseudorandom seeds. There is zero tile overlap, boundary overlap, or shared terrain mesh structure between the DEV and TEST sets. All hyperparameter tuning was conducted strictly on the DEV set.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Gradient Hazard Scoring}
The baseline module quantifies terrain slope and roughness at the pixel level by computing the spatial gradient of the input grayscale image $I(x,y)$. Horizontal and vertical partial derivatives are obtained via the Sobel operator \cite{sobel1968} with a standard $3 \times 3$ kernel size:
\begin{equation}
    I_x = \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}, \qquad I_y = \frac{\partial I}{\partial y}.
\end{equation}
The gradient magnitude at each pixel is defined as
\begin{equation}
    g(x,y) = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2},
\end{equation}
where high values capture abrupt intensity transitions associated with steep slopes, rock boundaries, or crater rims \cite{shepard2001roughness}. To suppress extreme specular outliers while maintaining scene-adaptive scaling, $g(x,y)$ is normalized by its 99th percentile $g_{p99}$ computed over non-zero pixels:
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y) = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{g(x,y)}{g_{p99}}\right).
\end{equation}
The bounded hazard score $G(x,y) \in [0,1]$ serves as the continuous predictor of unsafe terrain, where values near 1.0 denote high hazard and values near 0.0 denote smooth topography.

\subsection{Shadow Augmentation}
Under single-frame illumination, raw gradient magnitude fails inside broad, flat crater interiors and deeply shadowed depressions. Because illumination inside shadows is near uniform or zero, spatial intensity gradients vanish ($\nabla I \approx 0$), resulting in dangerously low hazard scores for steep, hazardous terrain.

To address this, a shadow augmentation module identifies dark connected regions using an inverse binary threshold at intensity level $\tau = 40$. External contours are extracted, and spurious small patches below $A_{\min} = 500$ pixels are discarded. For every retained contour, its enclosed interior is flood-filled, and the hazard score is set to maximum:
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y) \leftarrow 1.0 \quad \forall\,(x,y) \in \text{filled shadow region}.
\end{equation}
This reflects the physical fact that shadows adjacent to steep rims and crater walls indicate severe hazards \cite{he2010edge}.

\subsection{Safe-Region Fusion and Landing Box Selection}
The continuous safety score is the complement of the augmented gradient hazard: $s = 1 - G$. A binary safety mask is produced by thresholding $s > \theta$, with $\theta = 0.2$ tuned on the DEV validation set. Connected components smaller than 100 pixels are filtered out. Finally, a maximal-rectangle algorithm \cite{vandevoorde1998maximal} extracts the largest contiguous safe landing bounding box within the binary mask.

\subsection{Footprint Requirement as an Operating Parameter}
\label{sec:footprint}
The extracted landing box is parameterized by $B = (x, y, w, h)$ with area $A_{\text{box}} = w \cdot h$ (in pixels). Rather than fixing a single lander footprint, we treat the required footprint side length $L$ (in metres) as an operating parameter. At 1.0~m GSD, the area requirement is $A_{\text{req}}(L) = L^2$ pixels, and we define
\begin{equation}
    \text{Footprint OK}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(A_{\text{box}} \ge A_{\text{req}}(L)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. Because an area criterion alone admits elongated boxes that cannot physically contain an $L \times L$ touchdown zone, we additionally report the stricter square-fit criterion
\begin{equation}
    \text{Square Fit}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\min(w, h) \ge L\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, a box that is large enough but encloses unsafe terrain is not a usable site. We therefore define the \emph{safe landing rate}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Safe Landing}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\text{Square Fit}(L) \;\wedge\; f_{\text{unsafe}}(B) \le \epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{unsafe}}(B)$ is the fraction of proxy-unsafe pixels inside $B$ and $\epsilon = 0.05$. We sweep $L \in \{20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50\}$~m. The upper value, $L = 50$~m, corresponds to a large-lander requirement covering leg span, structural clearance, and touchdown dispersion; smaller values correspond to compact landers, rotorcraft, and landers with tighter navigation dispersion.

\textbf{Computation of PR-AUC and the Random Baseline:} In Table~\ref{tab:classification}, PR-AUC and AUROC are computed across pooled test pixels. Under this pooled definition, the random baseline ($B_5$) achieves a PR-AUC of 0.095 and precision of 0.087, matching the 9.5\% hazard prevalence of the held-out test set. If PR-AUC is instead computed as an unweighted macro-average over individual tiles, tiles containing zero hazards (smooth plains and dunes) yield undefined PR-AUC values ($0/0$) and must be discarded. Restricting the average to the 40 hazard-containing tiles (craters and rocky terrain) inflates the evaluated hazard prevalence to 19.0\%, causing the macro-averaged random PR-AUC to read 0.190 and $V_{\text{grad}}$ to read 0.201. Global pixel pooling avoids this sample-selection distortion and shows that $V_{\text{grad}}$ (PR-AUC 0.088) performs below chance (0.095).

\textbf{Physical Inversion and Its Correction:} Unaugmented gradient scoring ($V_{\text{grad}}$) achieves an AUROC of 0.350, below random guessing (0.500). Under single-frame Lambertian illumination, the steepest crater walls and bowl interiors cast dark shadows where spatial gradients vanish ($\nabla I \approx 0$), while benign plains exhibit surface roughness textures that produce high gradients. Intensity gradients therefore anti-correlate with physical slope in crater topography. Morphological shadow augmentation ($V_{\text{grad+shadow}}$) removes this failure mode, raising AUROC to 0.796 and PR-AUC to 0.559, nearly six times the chance level and roughly five times the best classical baseline (B1, 0.116). The correction costs a single threshold-and-fill operation and requires no learned parameters.

\section{Discussion: Where Single-Frame 2D Sensing Fits}
The results define a usable operating envelope for single-frame 2D hazard detection rather than a binary verdict on it.

\textbf{Small-footprint vehicles.} For footprint requirements up to 9~m, the pipeline returns a region that fits the footprint with at most 5\% unsafe terrain on at least half of the tiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}). The envelope is narrow: at 20~m and 25~m the rate falls to 21.2\% and 7.5\% (Table~\ref{tab:envelope}). Only vehicles whose required clear footprint, including touchdown dispersion, is of order 10~m, such as small rotorcraft, fall inside it and could use a camera-only hazard map with no additional sensor hardware; compact landers that need 20--25~m largely do not.

\textbf{Cueing for 3D sensors.} With AUROC 0.796 and PR-AUC 0.559, the augmented hazard map ranks terrain well above chance at very low cost. On vehicles that do carry lidar or stereo, it can act as a pre-filter that directs the 3D sensor toward the most promising regions, reducing the area that must be scanned at full resolution. This coarse-optical, fine-3D architecture is the one flown on Chang'e-3 \cite{li2016change3}.

\textbf{Degraded-mode fallback.} Because the front-end needs only a camera, under 100~ms, and under 16~MB, it is a candidate fallback when a primary 3D sensor degrades or fails.

\textbf{Outside the envelope.} Above 31~m no selected region can contain the footprint, and between 20~m and 31~m the safe landing rate is at most 21.2\%. For these footprints, including the 50~m large-lander requirement, single-frame 2D sensing alone does not deliver usable sites in our evaluation, and direct 3D measurement remains necessary.

\subsection{Limitations}
The operating envelope above is bounded by several intrinsic limitations of single-frame 2D hazard estimation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tilt and Shading Ambiguities:} Monocular intensity statistics cannot distinguish uniformly sloped, planar surfaces from level plains because both yield near-zero intensity gradients.
    \item \textbf{Illumination Sensitivity:} Shading depends on solar incidence and phase angles. Slopes oriented perpendicular to incident light can appear deceptively bright, while benign dips can cast sharp shadows. All results here use a single illumination geometry (azimuth $45^\circ$, elevation $30^\circ$), and the envelope is expected to shift with sun angle.
    \item \textbf{Footprint Fragmentation:} Conservative shadow and gradient thresholding fragments safe zones into irregular, elongated patches whose largest inscribed square is much smaller than their area suggests, which is what limits the method to small footprints.
    \item \textbf{Residual Contamination:} Averaged over all 80 test tiles, $V_{\text{grad+shadow}}$ boxes contain 18.6\% unsafe terrain. Because the 40 smooth and dune tiles contain no proxy-unsafe pixels, this average understates the problem on hazardous terrain: over the 40 crater and rocky tiles the mean is 37.1\%, every rocky box exceeds the 5\% tolerance, and 12 of 20 crater boxes are at least 89\% unsafe. The maximal-rectangle step uses only the binary safe mask, not the hazard score within it, so usability at a given footprint must be judged with the Safe Landing criterion rather than box size alone.
    \item \textbf{Proxy Label Fidelity:} Synthetic renders and DTM-derived labels approximate real terrain and do not capture small-scale rock distributions or soil strength required for landing gear certification. Validation on real orbital imagery is needed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This study characterizes the operational bounds of single-frame, intensity-based hazard detection for planetary landing, using unsafe terrain as the primary evaluation class and strict geographic separation between development and test terrain. Raw intensity gradients invert in shadowed craters (AUROC 0.350, PR-AUC 0.088); a lightweight shadow augmentation step corrects this, reaching AUROC 0.796 and PR-AUC 0.559, with a front-end that runs in 74.0~ms using under 16~MB of memory.

Treating the lander footprint as an operating parameter shows that the usefulness of 2D optical sensing depends strongly on vehicle size. The safe landing rate reaches 50\% only for footprints up to 9~m, falls to 7.5\% at a $25\text{ m} \times 25\text{ m}$ requirement, and is zero above 31~m; no tile supports a $50\text{ m} \times 50\text{ m}$ requirement, and on rocky terrain no selected region meets the 5\% contamination tolerance at any footprint. Single-frame 2D sensing is therefore a simple, low-cost option only for vehicles with footprints of order 10~m, and otherwise a cueing or fallback stage for 3D sensors, while larger vehicles continue to require direct 3D measurement. Future work will extend the envelope along the illumination axis, evaluate on real HiRISE imagery, and investigate footprint-aware site selection that targets the vehicle's required footprint directly rather than the maximal rectangle.
