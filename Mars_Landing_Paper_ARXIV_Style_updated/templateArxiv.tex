\begin{abstract}
Onboard hazard detection for planetary landing must run on radiation-hardened processors with tight compute and memory budgets. Single-frame 2D optical sensing is the simplest and cheapest sensing option available, and passive optical hazard avoidance has flown on recent lunar landers. We characterize what a deterministic, single-frame classical pipeline can deliver, and at what computational cost. The pipeline combines three image cues, intensity gradients, morphological shadow detection and a photometric slope cue, and scales the gradient and brightness cues by physical constants derived from the sun elevation and the slope threshold rather than by statistics of each image. We evaluate it against slope-derived proxy labels (unsafe: slope $>15^\circ$) on a synthetic Martian dataset rendered with Lambertian shading and ray-marched cast shadows, and on 160 tiles of real HiRISE orthoimages with co-registered terrain models from eight sites. On held-out synthetic sites the full hazard map reaches AUROC 0.866 and PR-AUC 0.316, 3.9 times the chance level, and the front-end runs in 28.5~ms per $768 \times 768$ tile on a single CPU core with about 30~MB of additional memory. Treating the lander footprint as an explicit operating parameter, the pipeline returns a safe landing site, a footprint-sized square with at most 5\% unsafe terrain, on 75\% of held-out tiles for every footprint from 5~m to 157~m: on every crater, plain and dune tile, while the rocky tiles contain no safe 20~m site at all. With parameters transferred unchanged from synthetic data, the hazard map ranks real HiRISE terrain above a local-texture baseline at three of four held-out sites (AUROC up to 0.80); on that terrain, where hazards are sparse, its selected sites are safe at about the rate of a randomly placed square. Single-frame classical vision is therefore an efficient hazard detector and cueing stage, and we quantify where its operating envelope ends.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous planetary landers operate under extreme communication latency, necessitating onboard hazard detection and avoidance systems that execute on radiation-hardened flight processors with severe computational constraints. While deep learning methods excel in terrestrial computer vision, classical lightweight vision pipelines remain strong candidates for spaceflight deployment because they are deterministic, interpretable, and cheap to verify. Landing-site selection for missions such as the Mars Science Laboratory relied on extensive orbital reconnaissance and terrain analysis prior to entry \cite{golombek2012selection}, while real-time onboard hazard detection has been pursued through lidar-based sensing \cite{amzajerdian2013lidar}, learning-based detection \cite{moghe2020deep}, and vision-based navigation systems such as the Mars 2020 Lander Vision System \cite{johnson2020lvs} and NASA's next-generation hazard detection system \cite{restrepo2020hds}. Radiation-hardened flight processors, such as the BAE RAD750 \cite{berger2001rad750}, provide limited compute throughput and memory bandwidth.

3D sensors such as lidar measure slope directly but add mass, power, and integration cost. A single camera, often already carried for terrain-relative navigation, is the simplest alternative. The practical question is therefore not whether 2D optical sensing is as good as 3D sensing, but \emph{under which operating conditions} it is good enough. Two conditions matter most: the terrain and illumination encountered, and the size of the hazard-free footprint the vehicle needs. A small lander or rotorcraft that needs a few tens of metres of clear ground places very different demands on a hazard map than a large lander that needs $50$~m or more.

Hazard detection algorithms are frequently evaluated against proxy ground truth derived from orbital Digital Terrain Models (DTMs). Such evaluations require careful class definition: treating the dominant safe terrain as the positive class allows high accuracy by trivially predicting the majority class and masks dangerous false negatives. We therefore evaluate unsafe terrain as the primary class throughout.

This paper characterizes the operating envelope of single-frame, intensity-based hazard scoring. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A leak-free evaluation protocol with site-level separation between independently generated development and test terrain, and unsafe terrain as the primary evaluation class.
    \item A three-cue classical hazard map (intensity gradient, shadow, and a photometric slope cue) whose gradient and brightness cues are scaled by physical constants from the sun elevation and the slope threshold, with an analysis of what each cue sees under oblique illumination.
    \item A footprint-requirement sweep that maps landing-site availability as a function of the required lander footprint and terrain type, and an ablation showing that per-image gradient normalization, not the cues themselves, had limited earlier versions to small footprints.
    \item An evaluation on real HiRISE orthoimages with co-registered terrain models from eight sites, with parameters transferred unchanged from the synthetic data.
    \item Per-stage latency and memory profiles showing that the front-end fits comfortably within flight-class compute budgets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Passive optical hazard detection.} The closest prior work is the multi-cue hazard detection system of Huertas, Cheng and Madison \cite{huertas2006passive}, which combines shadow, crater, slope and texture cues extracted from passive imagery to detect landing hazards for Mars and other landers. Our pipeline is a deliberately minimal member of this family: a gradient cue, a shadow cue and a brightness cue, each a single image-wide operation, chosen so that their cost and failure modes can be measured in isolation. Shadow analysis is also an established orbital tool: automated detection of rock shadows in HiRISE images was used to estimate rock abundance and select the Phoenix landing site \cite{golombek2008rocks}, a setting where illumination geometry is known and processing time is not constrained.

\textbf{Shape from shading and photoclinometry.} Recovering surface orientation from image brightness under a known illumination is the shape-from-shading problem \cite{horn1970shape,zhang1999sfs}; in planetary science the same principle, photoclinometry, relies on photometric models of the surface \cite{mcewen1991photometric}. Photoclinometry was used to measure metre-scale slopes and produce high-resolution terrain models for the candidate Mars Exploration Rover landing sites \cite{beyer2003meter,kirk2003topomapping}, with known limitations: a single image constrains mainly the slope component along the sun direction, and albedo variations are misread as slope. Our brightness cue is a deliberately minimal, first-order form of this idea. It does not integrate a surface; it only flags pixels whose local brightness departs from that of level ground, which costs one box filter and one median per image.

\textbf{Flown optical hazard avoidance.} Single-camera hazard detection has already flown. Chang'e-3 used an optical coarse hazard avoidance stage during powered descent, followed by fine avoidance with a 3D laser imaging sensor while hovering \cite{li2016change3}; single-image methods for selecting landing areas in this optical coarse stage are described in \cite{wei2018landing}. Chandrayaan-3 carried a Lander Hazard Detection and Avoidance Camera with an onboard processing algorithm among its lander sensors \cite{isro2023ch3}. These missions establish that passive optical sensing is operationally useful for coarse avoidance; our study does not argue otherwise, but quantifies, for one simple classical pipeline, how that usefulness depends on the required landing footprint and what it costs computationally.

\textbf{3D sensing.} Lidar-based hazard detection, developed in NASA's ALHAT project \cite{epp2008alhat,amzajerdian2013lidar} and its successors \cite{restrepo2020hds}, measures slope and roughness directly and remains the reference approach for precise avoidance. For planetary rotorcraft, Schoppmann et al. \cite{schoppmann2021multi} build multi-resolution elevation maps from monocular structure-from-motion and select landing sites by slope and roughness; this recovers 3D structure from multiple frames, whereas our pipeline uses a single frame and no motion.

\textbf{Learning-based hazard detection.} Convolutional networks have been trained to segment hazards for lunar landing \cite{moghe2020deep}, and Bayesian segmentation networks add per-pixel uncertainty that can be used to filter candidate sites, evaluated on terrain simulated from HiRISE DTMs \cite{tomita2022bayesian}. These methods are typically more accurate than hand-designed cues but require training data and substantially more onboard computation. Our focus is the opposite end of this trade-off: a deterministic pipeline with no learned parameters, whose accuracy we report alongside its runtime and memory footprint.

\section{Dataset and Terrain Independence}
We evaluate our method on a synthetic Martian dataset comprising 160 rendered tiles ($768 \times 768$ pixels at 1.0~m Ground Sampling Distance) across four distinct geological site types: craters, rocky terrain, smooth plains, and dunes. The topography is procedurally generated (crater bowls, rock fields, tilted plains, and sinusoidal dunes with small-scale noise) at the 1~m post spacing of high-resolution orbital DTMs such as HiRISE \cite{mcewen2007hirise}.

\textbf{Crater model.} Each crater tile contains one fresh or moderately degraded simple crater with rim-crest diameter $D$ drawn uniformly from 200--600~m (210--592~m in the dataset) and depth-to-diameter ratio $d/D$ drawn uniformly from 0.10 to 0.20, the range from degraded to fresh simple craters \cite{pike1977size,melosh1989impact}. The interior is a paraboloidal bowl, $z(r) = h_r - d\,(1 - r^2/R^2)$ for $r < R = D/2$, with rim height $h_r = 0.04\,D$ and an ejecta blanket $z(r) = h_r (R/r)^3$ outside the rim. Depths therefore range from 25 to 108~m. The wall is flat at the centre and steepest at the rim, where its slope is $\arctan(4\,d/D)$, between $22^\circ$ and $39^\circ$. Small-scale height noise (standard deviation 0.1~m per 1~m post) gives the surface a texture.

\textbf{Image model.} Images are rendered with unit-albedo Lambertian shading, $I = \max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s})$, for a sun at elevation $30^\circ$ and azimuth $45^\circ$ (measured in image coordinates from the $+x$ axis toward $+y$, so the sun lies toward the lower right of every image). Cast shadows are computed by ray marching the DTM toward the sun, and shadowed pixels are set to zero; atmospheric skylight is not modelled. No other darkening is applied. At $30^\circ$ elevation a crater casts a shadow only if its rim slope exceeds $30^\circ$, i.e.\ $d/D > 0.144$. The shadow then forms a crescent just inside the sun-side rim, while the far wall, which faces the sun, is the brightest part of the scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:crater_model}). On the test split, 9 of the 20 craters cast a shadow, covering on average 5.4\% and at most 16.6\% of the crater area. Under this model a planar slope of uniform albedo has nearly uniform intensity, so intensity gradients detect it only through its edges and its surface texture; its absolute brightness, however, still depends on its tilt toward or away from the sun, which both the gradient normalization and the cue of Section~\ref{sec:bright} exploit.

\textbf{Labels.} Proxy hazard labels are defined from the local gradient of the underlying DTM: any pixel with a maximum local slope exceeding $15^\circ$ over a 5~m baseline is labeled proxy-unsafe. Across the dataset, the average proxy-safe fraction is 91.7\%, meaning the hazardous unsafe class has a baseline prevalence of 8.3\% (and 8.1\% on the held-out test split). Smooth and dune tiles contain no proxy-unsafe pixels; on crater and rocky tiles the prevalence is 16.3\% and 16.7\%.

\textbf{Terrain Independence:} To ensure leak-free evaluation, train/validation and test independence is guaranteed via site-level partitioning rather than random patch shuffling. The development split (DEV, 80 tiles) is drawn exclusively from four synthetic sites (\texttt{crater\_1}, \texttt{rocky\_1}, \texttt{smooth\_1}, and \texttt{dunes\_1}). The held-out test split (TEST, 80 tiles) comprises four separate synthetic sites (\texttt{crater\_2}, \texttt{rocky\_2}, \texttt{smooth\_2}, and \texttt{dunes\_2}), produced by the same procedural generator with independent pseudorandom seeds. There is zero tile overlap, boundary overlap, or shared terrain mesh structure between the DEV and TEST sets. The split therefore tests generalization across independently generated terrain instances, not across geographic regions or data sources. All hyperparameters were tuned on a subset of 8 DEV tiles, two per terrain type; no TEST tile was used for tuning.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Gradient Hazard Scoring}
The gradient module responds to changes in surface orientation, which under oblique illumination appear as intensity transitions at rock boundaries, crater rims and slope breaks. Because the hazard labels are defined over a 5~m baseline, we measure the gradient at the same scale: the grayscale image $I(x,y)$ is first box-filtered over $b \times b$ pixels ($b = 5$, the label baseline at 1~m GSD), giving $I_b$, and horizontal and vertical derivatives are then obtained with the $3 \times 3$ Sobel operator \cite{sobel1968}:
\begin{equation}
    g(x,y) = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial I_b}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial I_b}{\partial y}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
We normalize $g$ by a fixed physical constant rather than by a statistic of the tile. For a Lambertian surface whose level ground has brightness $I_{\text{ref}}$ at sun elevation $e$, a facet tilted toward or away from the sun by a small angle $\alpha$ changes the brightness by approximately $I_{\text{ref}} \cot(e) \sin\alpha$. A slope change equal to the label threshold ($15^\circ$) spread over the baseline therefore produces a Sobel response (8 times the intensity change per pixel) of
\begin{equation}
    g_{\text{ref}} = \frac{8\, I_{\text{ref}} \cot(e) \sin 15^\circ}{b},
    \qquad
    G(x,y) = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{g(x,y)}{g_{\text{ref}}}\right).
\end{equation}
We estimate $I_{\text{ref}}$ as the median of the image, which assumes that most of a landing tile is near level and absorbs the unknown albedo and camera gain; the sun elevation is known onboard from the ephemeris. For the synthetic tiles $g_{\text{ref}} \approx 91$. A per-tile normalization, such as dividing by the 99th percentile of $g$ as in earlier versions of this pipeline, forces about 1\% of every tile to the maximum hazard however flat it is, and on hazard-free terrain it stretches small-scale texture across the full hazard range; Section~\ref{sec:norm} quantifies the difference.

\subsection{Shadow Augmentation}
Under single-frame illumination, raw gradient magnitude fails inside shadowed depressions: because illumination inside a shadow is near uniform or zero, spatial intensity gradients vanish ($\nabla I \approx 0$), resulting in dangerously low hazard scores for steep, hazardous terrain.

To address this, a shadow augmentation module identifies dark connected regions using an inverse binary threshold at intensity level $\tau = 60$. External contours are extracted, and spurious small patches below $A_{\min} = 500$ pixels are discarded; both values were selected on the DEV tiles from $\tau \in \{20, 40, 60, 80\}$ and $A_{\min} \in \{100, 500\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:fusion}). The threshold responds to any dark region, not only to cast shadows: on crater tiles the retained regions also include the part of the bowl that faces away from the sun and is dimly lit (Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}d). For every retained contour, its enclosed interior is flood-filled, and the hazard score is set to maximum:
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y) \leftarrow 1.0 \quad \forall\,(x,y) \in \text{filled shadow region}.
\end{equation}
Shadows are a standard passive cue for landing hazards such as rocks and steep crater walls \cite{huertas2006passive,golombek2008rocks}.

\subsection{Photometric Slope Cue}
\label{sec:bright}
The same photometric relation gives a direct, first-order photoclinometric slope cue: the deviation of local brightness from that of level ground. With $\bar I$ the $k \times k$ box-filtered image, we define
\begin{equation}
    B(x,y) = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{|\bar I(x,y) - I_{\text{ref}}|}{I_{\text{ref}} \cot(e) \sin\varphi}\right),
\end{equation}
so that $B$ saturates for a facet tilted by $\varphi$ toward or away from the sun. Like $g_{\text{ref}}$, the scale depends only on the image median and the sun elevation, so the cue carries over between images without re-scaling. It sees only the slope component along the sun azimuth and is zero for slopes that run across the sun direction. The full hazard map is the pixelwise maximum of the shadow-augmented gradient hazard and the brightness cue,
\begin{equation}
    H(x,y) = \max\big(G(x,y),\, B(x,y)\big).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Safe-Region Fusion and Landing Box Selection}
\label{sec:fusion}
We evaluate three variants: $V_{\text{grad}}$ uses the gradient hazard $G$ alone, $V_{\text{grad+shadow}}$ the shadow-augmented gradient hazard, and $V_{\text{full}}$ the full map $H$. The continuous safety score is the complement of the variant's hazard map, e.g.\ $s = 1 - H$ for $V_{\text{full}}$. A binary safety mask is produced by thresholding $s > \theta$, connected components smaller than a minimum area are removed, and a maximal-rectangle algorithm \cite{vandevoorde1998maximal} extracts the largest contiguous safe landing box within the mask.

\textbf{Parameter selection.} All parameters are selected on the same 8 DEV tiles (two per terrain type) by the F1 score of the filtered safe mask against the proxy-safe labels, in three stages: $\theta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.35, 0.5\}$ and the minimum component area in $\{100, 500, 1500\}$ for $V_{\text{grad}}$; then $\tau$ and $A_{\min}$ with these fixed; then $\varphi \in \{5^\circ, 7.5^\circ, 10^\circ, 12.5^\circ, 15^\circ, 20^\circ\}$ and $k \in \{5, 9, 15\}$. The selected values are $\theta = 0.05$, a minimum component area of 100 pixels, $\tau = 60$, $A_{\min} = 500$, $\varphi = 10^\circ$ and $k = 15$. No TEST tile is used for selection.

\subsection{Footprint Requirement as an Operating Parameter}
\label{sec:footprint}
The extracted landing box is parameterized by $B = (x, y, w, h)$ with area $A_{\text{box}} = w \cdot h$ (in pixels). Rather than fixing a single lander footprint, we treat the required footprint side length $L$ (in metres) as an operating parameter. At 1.0~m GSD, one pixel corresponds to one metre and the area requirement is $A_{\text{req}}(L) = L^2$ pixels. The envelope is therefore defined in pixels: at a different GSD, or with a different slope baseline than the 5~m used for the labels, the metre values scale accordingly. We define
\begin{equation}
    \text{Footprint OK}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(A_{\text{box}} \ge A_{\text{req}}(L)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. Because an area criterion alone admits elongated boxes that cannot physically contain an $L \times L$ touchdown zone, we additionally report the stricter square-fit criterion
\begin{equation}
    \text{Square Fit}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\min(w, h) \ge L\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, a box that is large enough but encloses unsafe terrain is not a usable site. We therefore define the \emph{safe landing rate}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Safe Landing}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\text{Square Fit}(L) \;\wedge\; f_{\text{unsafe}}(B) \le \epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{unsafe}}(B)$ is the fraction of proxy-unsafe pixels inside $B$ and $\epsilon = 0.05$. We sweep $L$ from 5~m to 200~m. The upper value, $L = 50$~m, corresponds to a large-lander requirement covering leg span, structural clearance, and touchdown dispersion; smaller values correspond to compact landers, rotorcraft, and landers with tighter navigation dispersion.

\textbf{Computation of PR-AUC and the Random Baseline:} In Table~\ref{tab:classification}, PR-AUC and AUROC are computed across pooled test pixels. Under this pooled definition, the random baseline ($B_5$) achieves a PR-AUC of 0.081, matching the 8.1\% hazard prevalence of the held-out test set; $B_5$ flags each tile at the same rate as $V_{\text{grad}}$. If PR-AUC is instead computed as an unweighted macro-average over individual tiles, tiles containing zero hazards (smooth plains and dunes) yield undefined PR-AUC values ($0/0$) and must be discarded. Restricting the average to the 40 hazard-containing tiles (craters and rocky terrain) inflates the evaluated hazard prevalence to 16.2\%, so the macro-averaged random PR-AUC reads 0.162. Global pixel pooling avoids this sample-selection distortion.

\textbf{What each cue captures:} Gradient scoring alone ($V_{\text{grad}}$) reaches a pooled AUROC of 0.687, but on crater tiles it barely separates unsafe from safe pixels (mean within-tile AUROC 0.497 over the 20 crater test tiles). Two effects combine. Inside a cast shadow the intensity is uniformly zero, so the gradient vanishes ($\nabla I \approx 0$). More importantly, a crater wall is a smooth slope whose shading changes gradually, and the wall that faces the sun is lit close to normal incidence, where Lambertian shading changes least with orientation. The mean gradient score on unsafe, sun-facing crater pixels is 0.116, no higher than the 0.120 on safe terrain.

The shadow cue addresses the first effect. Cast shadows contain 4.9\% of the unsafe crater pixels at $30^\circ$ elevation; the dark-region mask also captures the dimly lit wall that faces away from the sun and covers 25.7\%. It raises the within-tile crater AUROC to 0.617 and the pooled AUROC to 0.743 (PR-AUC 0.206). The brightness cue addresses the second effect: the sun-facing wall is bright precisely because it is tilted toward the sun, and with the cue added, 84.8\% of the unsafe sun-facing crater pixels fall below the safety threshold $\theta$. With all three cues ($V_{\text{full}}$), the within-tile crater AUROC rises to 0.930 and the pooled AUROC to 0.866, with a PR-AUC of 0.316: 3.9 times the chance level, 2.1 times gradients alone and 2.5 times the best classical baseline (B1, 0.124). Each cue is a single image-wide filtering operation with no learned parameters.

A per-tile normalization sets about 1\% of every tile to the maximum hazard however flat the tile is, and stretches small-scale texture across the full hazard range. On hazard-free terrain this scatters unsafe pixels across the safe mask and fragments it, so no box is wider than 51~m and no tile supports a 50~m footprint. A fixed constant applied to the raw $3 \times 3$ response is worse, because at the pixel scale surface texture alone exceeds $g_{\text{ref}}$. Measuring the gradient at the label baseline and normalizing it by the physical constant removes the fragmentation: every hazard-free tile becomes a single safe region, and the envelope extends from 25~m to 157~m. The per-tile variant keeps a slightly higher PR-AUC (0.375 against 0.316), but it cannot support any footprint of 50~m or more.

\section{Evaluation on Real HiRISE Data}
\label{sec:hirise}
\textbf{Data.} We evaluated the pipeline on eight HiRISE digital terrain models at 1~m post spacing with their co-registered RED orthoimages, distributed through the Planetary Data System \cite{mcewen2007hirise}. Four sites form a real DEV split (Gale crater, the InSight candidate site in Elysium Planitia, Holden crater and Mawrth Vallis) and four a real TEST split (Jezero crater, Eberswalde crater, Arcadia Planitia and the Meridiani Planum dunes). From each site we drew 20 random valid $768 \times 768$ tiles, giving 160 tiles. Orthoimages are linearly stretched to 8 bits, labels are slopes above $15^\circ$ over 5 DTM posts as for the synthetic data, and the sun elevation of each observation is read from its PDS label ($26^\circ$ to $57^\circ$; $33^\circ$ to $49^\circ$ at the TEST sites). The unsafe prevalence is 2.1\% at the DEV sites and 8.6\% at the TEST sites.

\textbf{Protocol.} In the main protocol all parameters are transferred unchanged from the synthetic DEV selection; only the image median and the sun elevation enter $g_{\text{ref}}$ and the brightness scale. As a check, we also re-selected all parameters on 5 tiles from each real DEV site with the same procedure and grids. Site selection is compared with a random-square null: the probability that a uniformly placed $L \times L$ square on the same tile contains at most 5\% unsafe pixels.

\textbf{Ranking.} The cues transfer to real imagery (Table~\ref{tab:hirise}). Without any tuning on real data, the brightness cue adds 0.049 AUROC and 0.060 within-tile AUROC over $V_{\text{grad+shadow}}$, and the physical gradient normalization improves on the per-tile one (0.654 against 0.622). Re-selecting all parameters on the real DEV sites gives no further gain (0.661), so the physically scaled parameters carry over from synthetic to real images. $V_{\text{full}}$ ranks terrain above the local-std baseline in pooled AUROC (0.654 against 0.643) and within tiles (0.704 against 0.658), and at three of the four sites by 0.13 to 0.20 AUROC (Table~\ref{tab:hirise_sites}). The pooled PR-AUC is below B1 (0.134 against 0.158) because of Arcadia Planitia, the site with the highest prevalence (15\%) and the highest sun, where both scores are weak. The shadow cue adds little at these sun elevations, and at the DEV sites Holden and Mawrth, where dark-toned material falls below $\tau$, it lowers AUROC (0.486 against 0.582 for $V_{\text{grad}}$).

\textbf{Site selection.} Real hazards are sparse, and every real TEST tile contains a safe $50\text{ m} \times 50\text{ m}$ square, so a randomly placed square is safe on 78\% of tiles at 20~m and 73\% at 50~m. $V_{\text{full}}$ returns a safe site on 80\% and 68\% of tiles: at the random rate overall, above it at Eberswalde (0.80 against 0.67 at 50~m) and Jezero at 20~m, and below it at Arcadia. The maximal rectangle selects the largest region the mask leaves rather than the region with the lowest hazard, so a better ranking does not translate directly into better sites; footprint-aware selection that uses the hazard score is the natural next step.

\textbf{Caveats.} The effective resolution of HiRISE DTMs is coarser than their 1~m post spacing, so the labels are smoothed and miss metre-scale rocks; the images are orbital, not descent, imagery; and the TEST split has four sites.

\section{Discussion: Where Single-Frame 2D Sensing Fits}
The results define an operating envelope for single-frame classical hazard detection, show which cue covers which part of the terrain, and test how far that carries to real imagery.

\textbf{Efficient front-end.} The full hazard map costs 28.5~ms per $768 \times 768$ tile on one CPU core and about 30~MB of additional memory, with no learned parameters and fully deterministic behaviour. The shadow and brightness cues together add 11.2~ms and double the PR-AUC of gradients alone. The front-end scales linearly with pixel count (Table~\ref{tab:runtime_breakdown}); timing on a flight processor remains to be measured.

\textbf{Physically scaled cues.} Normalizing the gradient and brightness cues by constants derived from the sun elevation and the slope threshold, rather than by statistics of each image, is what makes the pipeline usable for large footprints and across images: it removes the fragmentation that limited per-tile normalization to footprints below 50~m (Table~\ref{tab:norm}), keeps the cues valid across sun elevations without retuning (Table~\ref{tab:sun}), and lets parameters selected on synthetic data transfer to real HiRISE images with no loss (Table~\ref{tab:hirise}).

\textbf{Complementary cues.} The three cues respond to different physical signatures of the same hazard: gradients to edges and texture, shadows to the sun-averted side of steep slopes, and brightness to the sun-facing side. Gradients alone barely rank crater slopes (within-tile AUROC 0.497); all three together reach 0.930. Because all three depend on the known sun direction, their behaviour can be predicted for any planned landing time.

\textbf{Vehicles and footprints.} On the synthetic test set the pipeline returns a safe site on every crater, plain and dune tile for footprints up to 157~m, which covers small rotorcraft and large landers alike, and it correctly finds none on the rocky tiles. On real terrain its ranking transfers, while its selected sites match the safety of a random square; there, the 2D map is best used to rank candidate areas for a 3D sensor, the coarse-optical, fine-3D architecture flown on Chang'e-3 \cite{li2016change3}.

\textbf{Degraded-mode fallback.} Because the front-end needs only a camera, it is a candidate fallback when a primary 3D sensor degrades.

\subsection{Limitations}
The operating envelope above is bounded by several intrinsic limitations of single-frame 2D hazard estimation and by the simplifications of our data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Slope Direction Ambiguity:} Under Lambertian shading the brightness cue sees only the slope component along the sun azimuth, and intensity gradients see slopes mainly through their edges and texture. Slopes that run across the sun direction remain hard to detect from one image, the known limitation of single-image photoclinometry \cite{beyer2003meter}.
    \item \textbf{Illumination Sensitivity:} The dark threshold $\tau$ is an absolute grey level; at $15^\circ$ sun elevation it approaches the brightness of level ground and flags benign terrain (Section~\ref{sec:sun}). The other two constants follow the sun elevation; $\tau$ should too.
    \item \textbf{Albedo and Reference Brightness:} Both physical constants assume uniform albedo and estimate the level-ground brightness as the tile median. Albedo variations are read as slope, and dark-toned material is read as shadow, as at the real Holden and Mawrth sites. Photometric models beyond Lambert's law \cite{mcewen1991photometric} and local albedo normalization are natural extensions.
    \item \textbf{Site Selection:} The maximal-rectangle step uses only the binary safe mask, not the hazard score within it. On the synthetic data the largest safe region is also a safe one, but on real terrain the selected sites are safe at the rate of a random square. Selection that targets the required footprint and minimizes the hazard score inside it is needed to turn better ranking into better sites.
    \item \textbf{Data Fidelity:} The synthetic terrain is idealised: one rotationally symmetric crater per crater tile, uniform albedo, Lambertian reflectance and no atmospheric skylight. The real evaluation uses orbital images and DTM-derived labels at an effective resolution coarser than 1~m, with four TEST sites. Neither captures the small-scale rock distribution or soil strength relevant to landing gear certification, or the geometry of descent imagery.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This study characterizes what single-frame classical hazard detection can deliver for planetary landing, on synthetic imagery rendered with Lambertian shading and ray-marched cast shadows and on real HiRISE orthoimages with co-registered terrain models. Three inexpensive cues, intensity gradients, shadows and a first-order photometric slope cue, complement each other, and scaling them by physical constants from the sun elevation and the slope threshold makes them robust across tiles, sun elevations and data sources. On held-out synthetic sites the full map reaches AUROC 0.866 and PR-AUC 0.316 in 28.5~ms per tile on a single CPU core with about 30~MB of additional memory, and it returns a safe site on every crater, plain and dune tile for footprints up to 157~m. On real HiRISE terrain, with parameters transferred unchanged, it ranks hazards above a local-texture baseline at three of four sites. Single-frame classical vision is therefore an efficient hazard detector and an inexpensive cueing stage for 3D sensors. Future work will add footprint-aware site selection that uses the hazard score, scale the dark threshold with the sun elevation, normalize local albedo, and evaluate on descent imagery.
