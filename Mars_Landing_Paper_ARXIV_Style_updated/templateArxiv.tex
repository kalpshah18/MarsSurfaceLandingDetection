\begin{abstract}
Onboard hazard detection for planetary landing must run on radiation-hardened processors with tight compute and memory budgets. Single-frame 2D optical sensing is the simplest and cheapest sensing option available, and passive optical hazard avoidance has flown on recent lunar landers. We characterize what a deterministic, single-frame classical pipeline can deliver, and at what computational cost. The pipeline combines three image cues: intensity gradients, morphological shadow detection, and a photometric slope cue that measures how far local brightness departs from that of level ground. We evaluate it against slope-derived proxy labels (unsafe: slope $>15^\circ$) on a synthetic Martian dataset rendered with Lambertian shading and ray-marched cast shadows, with disjoint development and test sites. Intensity gradients alone barely separate unsafe from safe terrain (AUROC 0.529), because a crater wall that faces the sun appears bright and nearly textureless. Shadow detection captures the sun-averted side of crater bowls (AUROC 0.598), and the brightness cue captures the sun-facing side: the three cues together reach AUROC 0.750 and PR-AUC 0.359, 4.4 times the 0.081 chance level and 3.7 times raw gradients. Across sun elevations from $15^\circ$ to $60^\circ$ the shadow cue dominates at low sun and the brightness cue at high sun, and the full map is the best variant at every elevation. The full hazard-map front-end runs in 31.5~ms per $768 \times 768$ tile on a single CPU core with about 30~MB of additional resident memory. Treating the lander footprint as an explicit operating parameter, the pipeline returns a safe landing region (a footprint-sized square with at most 5\% unsafe terrain) on every hazard-free test tile for footprints up to 5~m, on at least half of all test tiles up to 9~m, and on 26.3\% at 20~m. On crater terrain it finds safe sites on 30\% of tiles for footprints up to 15~m, where gradient and shadow cues alone find none. The remaining misses are slopes that run across the sun direction, the known limitation of single-image photoclinometry. Single-frame classical vision is therefore an efficient hazard detector for small-footprint landing and an inexpensive cueing stage for 3D sensors, and we quantify where its operating envelope ends.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous planetary landers operate under extreme communication latency, necessitating onboard hazard detection and avoidance systems that execute on radiation-hardened flight processors with severe computational constraints. While deep learning methods excel in terrestrial computer vision, classical lightweight vision pipelines remain strong candidates for spaceflight deployment because they are deterministic, interpretable, and cheap to verify. Landing-site selection for missions such as the Mars Science Laboratory relied on extensive orbital reconnaissance and terrain analysis prior to entry \cite{golombek2012selection}, while real-time onboard hazard detection has been pursued through lidar-based sensing \cite{amzajerdian2013lidar}, learning-based detection \cite{moghe2020deep}, and vision-based navigation systems such as the Mars 2020 Lander Vision System \cite{johnson2020lvs} and NASA's next-generation hazard detection system \cite{restrepo2020hds}. Radiation-hardened flight processors, such as the BAE RAD750 \cite{berger2001rad750}, provide limited compute throughput and memory bandwidth.

3D sensors such as lidar measure slope directly but add mass, power, and integration cost. A single camera, often already carried for terrain-relative navigation, is the simplest alternative. The practical question is therefore not whether 2D optical sensing is as good as 3D sensing, but \emph{under which operating conditions} it is good enough. Two conditions matter most: the terrain and illumination encountered, and the size of the hazard-free footprint the vehicle needs. A small lander or rotorcraft that needs a few tens of metres of clear ground places very different demands on a hazard map than a large lander that needs $50$~m or more.

Hazard detection algorithms are frequently evaluated against proxy ground truth derived from orbital Digital Terrain Models (DTMs). Such evaluations require careful class definition: treating the dominant safe terrain as the positive class allows high accuracy by trivially predicting the majority class and masks dangerous false negatives. We therefore evaluate unsafe terrain as the primary class throughout.

This paper characterizes the operating envelope of single-frame, intensity-based hazard scoring. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A leak-free evaluation protocol with site-level separation between independently generated development and test terrain, and unsafe terrain as the primary evaluation class.
    \item A three-cue classical hazard map (intensity gradient, shadow, and a photometric slope cue) with a physical analysis of what each cue sees under oblique illumination. Shadows capture the sun-averted side of crater bowls and brightness captures the sun-facing side, together raising the PR-AUC of raw gradients from 0.098 to 0.359 at negligible cost.
    \item A footprint-requirement sweep that maps landing-region availability as a function of the required lander footprint and terrain type, identifying where single-frame 2D sensing returns safe sites on its own and where it should cue a 3D sensor.
    \item Per-stage latency and memory profiles showing that the front-end fits comfortably within flight-class compute budgets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Passive optical hazard detection.} The closest prior work is the multi-cue hazard detection system of Huertas, Cheng and Madison \cite{huertas2006passive}, which combines shadow, crater, slope and texture cues extracted from passive imagery to detect landing hazards for Mars and other landers. Our pipeline is a deliberately minimal member of this family: a gradient cue, a shadow cue and a brightness cue, each a single image-wide operation, chosen so that their cost and failure modes can be measured in isolation. Shadow analysis is also an established orbital tool: automated detection of rock shadows in HiRISE images was used to estimate rock abundance and select the Phoenix landing site \cite{golombek2008rocks}, a setting where illumination geometry is known and processing time is not constrained.

\textbf{Shape from shading and photoclinometry.} Recovering surface orientation from image brightness under a known illumination is the shape-from-shading problem \cite{horn1970shape,zhang1999sfs}; in planetary science the same principle, photoclinometry, relies on photometric models of the surface \cite{mcewen1991photometric}. Photoclinometry was used to measure metre-scale slopes and produce high-resolution terrain models for the candidate Mars Exploration Rover landing sites \cite{beyer2003meter,kirk2003topomapping}, with known limitations: a single image constrains mainly the slope component along the sun direction, and albedo variations are misread as slope. Our brightness cue is a deliberately minimal, first-order form of this idea. It does not integrate a surface; it only flags pixels whose local brightness departs from that of level ground, which costs one box filter and one median per image.

\textbf{Flown optical hazard avoidance.} Single-camera hazard detection has already flown. Chang'e-3 used an optical coarse hazard avoidance stage during powered descent, followed by fine avoidance with a 3D laser imaging sensor while hovering \cite{li2016change3}; single-image methods for selecting landing areas in this optical coarse stage are described in \cite{wei2018landing}. Chandrayaan-3 carried a Lander Hazard Detection and Avoidance Camera with an onboard processing algorithm among its lander sensors \cite{isro2023ch3}. These missions establish that passive optical sensing is operationally useful for coarse avoidance; our study does not argue otherwise, but quantifies, for one simple classical pipeline, how that usefulness depends on the required landing footprint and what it costs computationally.

\textbf{3D sensing.} Lidar-based hazard detection, developed in NASA's ALHAT project \cite{epp2008alhat,amzajerdian2013lidar} and its successors \cite{restrepo2020hds}, measures slope and roughness directly and remains the reference approach for precise avoidance. For planetary rotorcraft, Schoppmann et al. \cite{schoppmann2021multi} build multi-resolution elevation maps from monocular structure-from-motion and select landing sites by slope and roughness; this recovers 3D structure from multiple frames, whereas our pipeline uses a single frame and no motion.

\textbf{Learning-based hazard detection.} Convolutional networks have been trained to segment hazards for lunar landing \cite{moghe2020deep}, and Bayesian segmentation networks add per-pixel uncertainty that can be used to filter candidate sites, evaluated on terrain simulated from HiRISE DTMs \cite{tomita2022bayesian}. These methods are typically more accurate than hand-designed cues but require training data and substantially more onboard computation. Our focus is the opposite end of this trade-off: a deterministic pipeline with no learned parameters, whose accuracy we report alongside its runtime and memory footprint.

\section{Dataset and Terrain Independence}
We evaluate our method on a synthetic Martian dataset comprising 160 rendered tiles ($768 \times 768$ pixels at 1.0~m Ground Sampling Distance) across four distinct geological site types: craters, rocky terrain, smooth plains, and dunes. The topography is procedurally generated (crater bowls, rock fields, tilted plains, and sinusoidal dunes with small-scale noise) at the 1~m post spacing of high-resolution orbital DTMs such as HiRISE \cite{mcewen2007hirise}.

\textbf{Crater model.} Each crater tile contains one fresh or moderately degraded simple crater with rim-crest diameter $D$ drawn uniformly from 200--600~m (210--592~m in the dataset) and depth-to-diameter ratio $d/D$ drawn uniformly from 0.10 to 0.20, the range from degraded to fresh simple craters \cite{pike1977size,melosh1989impact}. The interior is a paraboloidal bowl, $z(r) = h_r - d\,(1 - r^2/R^2)$ for $r < R = D/2$, with rim height $h_r = 0.04\,D$ and an ejecta blanket $z(r) = h_r (R/r)^3$ outside the rim. Depths therefore range from 25 to 108~m. The wall is flat at the centre and steepest at the rim, where its slope is $\arctan(4\,d/D)$, between $22^\circ$ and $39^\circ$. Small-scale height noise (standard deviation 0.1~m per 1~m post) gives the surface a texture.

\textbf{Image model.} Images are rendered with unit-albedo Lambertian shading, $I = \max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s})$, for a sun at elevation $30^\circ$ and azimuth $45^\circ$ (measured in image coordinates from the $+x$ axis toward $+y$, so the sun lies toward the lower right of every image). Cast shadows are computed by ray marching the DTM toward the sun, and shadowed pixels are set to zero; atmospheric skylight is not modelled. No other darkening is applied. At $30^\circ$ elevation a crater casts a shadow only if its rim slope exceeds $30^\circ$, i.e.\ $d/D > 0.144$. The shadow then forms a crescent just inside the sun-side rim, while the far wall, which faces the sun, is the brightest part of the scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:crater_model}). On the test split, 9 of the 20 craters cast a shadow, covering on average 5.4\% and at most 16.6\% of the crater area. Under this model a planar slope of uniform albedo has nearly uniform intensity, so intensity gradients detect it only through its edges and its surface texture; its absolute brightness, however, still depends on its tilt toward or away from the sun, which the cue of Section~\ref{sec:bright} exploits.

\textbf{Labels.} Proxy hazard labels are defined from the local gradient of the underlying DTM: any pixel with a maximum local slope exceeding $15^\circ$ over a 5~m baseline is labeled proxy-unsafe. Across the dataset, the average proxy-safe fraction is 91.7\%, meaning the hazardous unsafe class has a baseline prevalence of 8.3\% (and 8.1\% on the held-out test split). Smooth and dune tiles contain no proxy-unsafe pixels; on crater and rocky tiles the prevalence is 16.3\% and 16.7\%.

\textbf{Terrain Independence:} To ensure leak-free evaluation, train/validation and test independence is guaranteed via site-level partitioning rather than random patch shuffling. The development split (DEV, 80 tiles) is drawn exclusively from four synthetic sites (\texttt{crater\_1}, \texttt{rocky\_1}, \texttt{smooth\_1}, and \texttt{dunes\_1}). The held-out test split (TEST, 80 tiles) comprises four separate synthetic sites (\texttt{crater\_2}, \texttt{rocky\_2}, \texttt{smooth\_2}, and \texttt{dunes\_2}), produced by the same procedural generator with independent pseudorandom seeds. There is zero tile overlap, boundary overlap, or shared terrain mesh structure between the DEV and TEST sets. The split therefore tests generalization across independently generated terrain instances, not across geographic regions or data sources. All hyperparameters were tuned on a subset of 8 DEV tiles, two per terrain type; no TEST tile was used for tuning.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Gradient Hazard Scoring}
The baseline module quantifies terrain slope and roughness at the pixel level by computing the spatial gradient of the input grayscale image $I(x,y)$. Horizontal and vertical partial derivatives are obtained via the Sobel operator \cite{sobel1968} with a standard $3 \times 3$ kernel size:
\begin{equation}
    I_x = \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}, \qquad I_y = \frac{\partial I}{\partial y}.
\end{equation}
The gradient magnitude at each pixel is defined as
\begin{equation}
    g(x,y) = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2},
\end{equation}
where high values mark abrupt intensity transitions, which under oblique illumination occur at rock boundaries, crater rims, and changes in surface orientation. To suppress extreme specular outliers while maintaining scene-adaptive scaling, $g(x,y)$ is normalized by its 99th percentile $g_{p99}$ computed over non-zero pixels:
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y) = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{g(x,y)}{g_{p99}}\right).
\end{equation}
The bounded hazard score $G(x,y) \in [0,1]$ serves as the continuous predictor of unsafe terrain, where values near 1.0 denote high hazard and values near 0.0 denote smooth topography.

\subsection{Shadow Augmentation}
Under single-frame illumination, raw gradient magnitude fails inside shadowed depressions: because illumination inside a shadow is near uniform or zero, spatial intensity gradients vanish ($\nabla I \approx 0$), resulting in dangerously low hazard scores for steep, hazardous terrain.

To address this, a shadow augmentation module identifies dark connected regions using an inverse binary threshold at intensity level $\tau = 60$. External contours are extracted, and spurious small patches below $A_{\min} = 500$ pixels are discarded. Both values were selected on the DEV tiles from $\tau \in \{20, 40, 60, 80\}$ and $A_{\min} \in \{100, 500\}$ by the F1 score of the filtered safe mask against the proxy-safe labels. The threshold responds to any dark region, not only to cast shadows: on crater tiles the retained regions also include the part of the bowl that faces away from the sun and is dimly lit (Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}d). For every retained contour, its enclosed interior is flood-filled, and the hazard score is set to maximum:
\begin{equation}
    G(x,y) \leftarrow 1.0 \quad \forall\,(x,y) \in \text{filled shadow region}.
\end{equation}
Shadows are a standard passive cue for landing hazards such as rocks and steep crater walls \cite{huertas2006passive,golombek2008rocks}.

\subsection{Photometric Slope Cue}
\label{sec:bright}
For a Lambertian surface of uniform albedo, level ground has intensity proportional to $\sin e$, where $e$ is the sun elevation, and a facet tilted toward or away from the sun is brighter or darker. A first-order photoclinometric slope cue is therefore the deviation of local brightness from that of level ground. We estimate the level-ground brightness as the median $I_{\text{ref}}$ of the box-filtered image $\bar I$ (a $k \times k$ mean), which assumes that most of a landing tile is near level and absorbs the unknown albedo, and define
\begin{equation}
    B(x,y) = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{|\bar I(x,y) - I_{\text{ref}}|}{\kappa}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the brightness change that counts as fully hazardous. At $e = 30^\circ$, a $15^\circ$ tilt directly toward or away from the sun changes the intensity of an otherwise level surface by roughly 50--60 grey levels, so $\kappa$ of this order matches the label threshold. The cue sees only the slope component along the sun azimuth, and it is zero for slopes that run across the sun direction. The full hazard map is the pixelwise maximum of the shadow-augmented gradient hazard and the brightness cue,
\begin{equation}
    H(x,y) = \max\big(G(x,y),\, B(x,y)\big).
\end{equation}
With $\tau$, $A_{\min}$ and $\theta$ fixed, $\kappa \in \{20, 30, 40, 50, 70\}$ and $k \in \{5, 9, 15\}$ were selected on the same 8 DEV tiles by the same criterion, giving $\kappa = 50$ and $k = 15$.

\subsection{Safe-Region Fusion and Landing Box Selection}
We evaluate three variants: $V_{\text{grad}}$ uses the gradient hazard $G$ alone, $V_{\text{grad+shadow}}$ the shadow-augmented gradient hazard, and $V_{\text{full}}$ the full map $H$. The continuous safety score is the complement of the variant's hazard map, e.g.\ $s = 1 - H$ for $V_{\text{full}}$. A binary safety mask is produced by thresholding $s > \theta$, with $\theta = 0.2$ tuned on the same 8 DEV tiles. Connected components smaller than 100 pixels are filtered out. Finally, a maximal-rectangle algorithm \cite{vandevoorde1998maximal} extracts the largest contiguous safe landing bounding box within the binary mask.

\subsection{Footprint Requirement as an Operating Parameter}
\label{sec:footprint}
The extracted landing box is parameterized by $B = (x, y, w, h)$ with area $A_{\text{box}} = w \cdot h$ (in pixels). Rather than fixing a single lander footprint, we treat the required footprint side length $L$ (in metres) as an operating parameter. At 1.0~m GSD, one pixel corresponds to one metre and the area requirement is $A_{\text{req}}(L) = L^2$ pixels. The envelope is therefore defined in pixels: at a different GSD, or with a different slope baseline than the 5~m used for the labels, the metre values scale accordingly. We define
\begin{equation}
    \text{Footprint OK}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(A_{\text{box}} \ge A_{\text{req}}(L)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. Because an area criterion alone admits elongated boxes that cannot physically contain an $L \times L$ touchdown zone, we additionally report the stricter square-fit criterion
\begin{equation}
    \text{Square Fit}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\min(w, h) \ge L\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, a box that is large enough but encloses unsafe terrain is not a usable site. We therefore define the \emph{safe landing rate}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Safe Landing}(L) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\text{Square Fit}(L) \;\wedge\; f_{\text{unsafe}}(B) \le \epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{unsafe}}(B)$ is the fraction of proxy-unsafe pixels inside $B$ and $\epsilon = 0.05$. We sweep $L \in \{20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50\}$~m. The upper value, $L = 50$~m, corresponds to a large-lander requirement covering leg span, structural clearance, and touchdown dispersion; smaller values correspond to compact landers, rotorcraft, and landers with tighter navigation dispersion.

\textbf{Computation of PR-AUC and the Random Baseline:} In Table~\ref{tab:classification}, PR-AUC and AUROC are computed across pooled test pixels. Under this pooled definition, the random baseline ($B_5$) achieves a PR-AUC of 0.081, matching the 8.1\% hazard prevalence of the held-out test set; $B_5$ flags each tile at the same rate as $V_{\text{grad}}$. If PR-AUC is instead computed as an unweighted macro-average over individual tiles, tiles containing zero hazards (smooth plains and dunes) yield undefined PR-AUC values ($0/0$) and must be discarded. Restricting the average to the 40 hazard-containing tiles (craters and rocky terrain) inflates the evaluated hazard prevalence to 16.2\%, causing the macro-averaged random PR-AUC to read 0.162 and $V_{\text{grad}}$ to read 0.184. Global pixel pooling avoids this sample-selection distortion.

\textbf{What each cue captures:} Gradient scoring alone ($V_{\text{grad}}$) reaches a pooled AUROC of 0.529, and on crater tiles it ranks unsafe pixels below safe ones (mean within-tile AUROC 0.435 over the 20 crater test tiles). Two effects combine. Inside a cast shadow the intensity is uniformly zero, so the gradient vanishes ($\nabla I \approx 0$). More importantly, the wall that faces the sun is lit close to normal incidence, where Lambertian shading changes least with small changes in surface orientation; its texture produces little intensity contrast, and it appears bright and smooth. The mean raw gradient score on unsafe, sun-facing crater pixels is 0.254, below the 0.353 on safe terrain, while unsafe pixels elsewhere on the lit walls score 0.359.

The shadow cue addresses the first effect. Cast shadows contain 4.9\% of the unsafe crater pixels at $30^\circ$ elevation; the dark-region mask also captures the dimly lit wall that faces away from the sun and covers 25.7\%. It raises the within-tile crater AUROC to 0.575 and the pooled AUROC to 0.598 (PR-AUC 0.194). The brightness cue addresses the second effect: the sun-facing wall is bright precisely because it is tilted toward the sun, and with the cue added, 78.7\% of the unsafe sun-facing crater pixels fall below the safety threshold $\theta$. With all three cues ($V_{\text{full}}$), the within-tile crater AUROC rises to 0.875 and the pooled AUROC to 0.750, with a PR-AUC of 0.359: 4.4 times the chance level, 3.7 times raw gradients and 2.9 times the best classical baseline (B1, 0.124). Each cue is a single image-wide filtering operation with no learned parameters.

\section{Discussion: Where Single-Frame 2D Sensing Fits}
The results define an operating envelope for single-frame classical hazard detection and show which cue covers which part of the terrain.

\textbf{Efficient front-end.} The full hazard map costs 31.5~ms per $768 \times 768$ tile on one CPU core and about 30~MB of additional memory, with no learned parameters and fully deterministic behaviour. The shadow and brightness cues together add 12.1~ms and raise PR-AUC from 0.098 to 0.359. The front-end scales linearly with pixel count (Table~\ref{tab:runtime_breakdown}); timing on a flight processor remains to be measured.

\textbf{Complementary cues.} The three cues respond to different physical signatures of the same hazard. Gradients respond to texture and edges, shadows to the sun-averted side of steep slopes, and brightness to the sun-facing side. Each is weak alone on crater terrain (within-tile AUROC 0.435 for gradients), and together they reach 0.875. Because all three depend on the known sun direction, their behaviour can be predicted for any planned landing time.

\textbf{Small-footprint vehicles.} $V_{\text{full}}$ returns a safe site on every hazard-free test tile at 5~m, on at least half of all test tiles up to 9~m and on 26.3\% at 20~m (Fig.~\ref{fig:envelope}, Table~\ref{tab:envelope}), including 30\% of crater tiles up to 15~m. For small vehicles such as rotorcraft, a camera-only hazard map of this kind can supply candidate sites with no additional sensor hardware.

\textbf{Cueing for 3D sensors.} With AUROC 0.750 and PR-AUC 0.359, the full map ranks terrain well enough to act as a pre-filter that directs lidar or stereo, the coarse-optical, fine-3D architecture flown on Chang'e-3 \cite{li2016change3}. Its residual blind spot is also predictable: slopes that run across the sun direction. A cueing stage can assign these, which are identifiable from the sun azimuth and the local topographic context, to the 3D sensor or to a second image taken under different illumination or viewing geometry.

\textbf{Degraded-mode fallback.} Because the front-end needs only a camera, it is a candidate fallback when a primary 3D sensor degrades.

\textbf{Larger footprints.} From 31~m upward no tile yields a safe region, because the safe regions in the binary mask are fragmented and elongated. For these footprints, including the 50~m large-lander requirement, the 2D map is best used for cueing, with direct 3D measurement for site certification.

\subsection{Limitations}
The operating envelope above is bounded by several intrinsic limitations of single-frame 2D hazard estimation and by the simplifications of our synthetic data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Slope Direction Ambiguity:} Under Lambertian shading the brightness cue sees only the slope component along the sun azimuth, and intensity gradients see slopes only through their edges and texture. Slopes that run across the sun direction remain hard to detect from one image; they account for the remaining unsafe crater boxes.
    \item \textbf{Illumination Sensitivity:} Shading depends on solar incidence and phase angles. Cast shadows appear only on slopes steeper than the sun elevation, and the brightness change of a tilted facet depends on the sun elevation. Section~\ref{sec:sun} quantifies this for elevations of $15^\circ$ to $60^\circ$; the main results use a single geometry (azimuth $45^\circ$, elevation $30^\circ$) with parameters tuned for it.
    \item \textbf{Albedo and Reference Brightness:} The brightness cue assumes uniform albedo and estimates the level-ground brightness as the tile median, which assumes that most of the tile is near level. Albedo variations on real terrain would be read as slope, and a tile dominated by a single slope would bias the reference. Photometric models beyond Lambert's law \cite{mcewen1991photometric} and local albedo normalisation are natural extensions.
    \item \textbf{Footprint Fragmentation:} Surface texture fragments the safe mask into small, irregular patches; the largest box on any tile fits a square of at most 33~m, which limits the method to small footprints even on benign terrain.
    \item \textbf{Residual Contamination:} Averaged over all 80 test tiles, $V_{\text{full}}$ boxes contain 19.8\% unsafe terrain. Over the 40 crater and rocky tiles the mean is 39.7\%: every rocky box exceeds the 5\% tolerance, as does any selector at $L \ge 20$~m, and 14 of the 20 crater boxes exceed it. The maximal-rectangle step uses only the binary safe mask, not the hazard score within it, so usability at a given footprint must be judged with the Safe Landing criterion rather than box size alone.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Data Fidelity:} The terrain and image models are idealised: one rotationally symmetric crater per crater tile, uniform albedo, Lambertian reflectance, no atmospheric skylight in shadows, and no boulders, ejecta texture or albedo variation. Real Martian imagery adds albedo contrast, dust-scattered skylight and non-Lambertian photometry, each of which changes all three cues. DTM-derived labels also do not capture small-scale rock distributions or soil strength required for landing gear certification. Validation on real orbital imagery is needed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This study characterizes what single-frame classical hazard detection can deliver for planetary landing, using unsafe terrain as the primary evaluation class, strict site-level separation between independently generated development and test terrain, and imagery rendered with Lambertian shading and ray-marched cast shadows. Three inexpensive cues, intensity gradients, shadows and a first-order photometric slope cue, complement each other: gradients alone barely separate unsafe from safe terrain (AUROC 0.529), while all three together reach AUROC 0.750 and PR-AUC 0.359, 4.4 times chance, in 31.5~ms per tile on a single CPU core with about 30~MB of additional memory.

Treating the lander footprint as an operating parameter shows where this efficiency translates into usable sites. The full pipeline returns a safe site on at least half of all test tiles for footprints up to 9~m and on every hazard-free tile at 5~m, and it finds safe sites on crater terrain that gradient and shadow cues alone miss. Its remaining blind spot, slopes running across the sun direction, follows from the illumination geometry and can be anticipated and assigned to a 3D sensor. Single-frame classical vision is therefore an efficient hazard detector for small-footprint landing and an inexpensive cueing stage for larger vehicles. Future work will evaluate on real HiRISE imagery with local albedo normalisation, combine images taken under different illumination to resolve slopes across the sun direction, and investigate footprint-aware site selection on the full hazard map.
